<Callout type="info" title="Phase A Audit (2026-05-04)">

## 1. Statement

Let `M ∈ ℤ^{128×128}` be the Anna Matrix and define

> `T : {-1, 0, +1}^128 → {-1, 0, +1}^128, T(v) = sign(M · v)`

We verify, with Tier-1 evidence:

1. **(Three attractors total)** `T` admits one period-1 fixed point (the zero vector) and exactly **two** period-4 cycles. The zero vector is trivial and is never reached from random sampling; the two period-4 cycles are the global attractors of all non-zero ternary inputs.
2. **(Universality)** Every non-zero `v ∈ {-1,0,+1}^128` reaches one of the two period-4 cycles within ≤ 24 iterations (median 8 ticks-to-first-cycle-state, median 12 ticks-to-cycle-close).
3. **(Specificity)** Period-4 of this form is not generic for int8 matrices: under four distinct null ensembles (200 matrices each) Anna's behaviour is reproduced 0% – 0.5% of the time.
4. **(Period-4 fixed-point property)** Each of the 8 cycle states `s` satisfies `T⁴(s) = s` exactly. This is a tautological consequence of period-4 (the fourth iterate returns to the start) but is the property a 4-tick sampling regime would exploit.
5. **(Cycle-pair structure)** `T` commutes with negation: `T(-v) = -T(v)`. The orbit of `-A[0]` is therefore exactly `(-A[0], -A[1], -A[2], -A[3])`. This is cycle B: the antipodal cycle shifted by the choice of starting phase. There is no additional structural permutation; cycle B is just `-A` with the orbit origin re-indexed.

## 2. Methods (Phase A reproducibility)

All computations live in __. Scripts: `phase_a.py` (~15 s) and `phase_a_hardening.py` (~80 s). Outputs: JSON files in __.

### 2.1 Cycle detection

A hash-based detector iterates `T` from `v_0` and records every state via `bytes(v.tobytes())`. Termination on first repeat or at `max_ticks = 100`. The cycle period is the index difference between the first occurrence and the repetition.

### 2.2 Random-matrix null ensembles

Four distinct null hypotheses, each generating 200 matrices and testing 200 ternary inputs per matrix (40,000 input-matrix pairs per null):

| Null | Construction | Question answered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `null_uniform` | each entry i.i.d. Uniform(int8) | Is period-4 generic for any int8 matrix? |
| `null_histogram` | Anna's value multiset, randomly permuted | Is the *arrangement* the cause? |
| `null_phase_random` | eigendecompose M, randomise every conjugate-pair phase, reconstruct + clip | Is the spectral magnitude profile alone enough? |
| `null_phase_keep_pi2` | as `null_phase_random` but keep dominant pair at exactly π/2 | Does π/2 *alone* suffice? |

The fourth null was added on advisor's recommendation as the strongest test of the dominant phase as the causal factor.

### 2.3 Two convergence definitions

- **`ttfc`** (ticks to first cycle state) — when the orbit first enters the cycle.
- **`ttcc`** (ticks to cycle close) — when we first see a state we've already visited (i.e. observed the full period). `ttcc = ttfc + 4` for period-4 orbits.

This reconciles our earlier "median 8" with the Companion PDF's "≈ 11": both correct, different definitions.

## 5. Spectral motivation

Top eigenvalues of `M`:

| Index | Magnitude | Phase (deg) | Distance to 90° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 2342.075 | +90.4565 | 0.4565° |
| 1 | 2342.075 | -90.4565 | (conjugate) |
| 2 | 884.008 | 180 | 90° |
| 3 | 876.576 | 0 | 90° |

Spectral dominance |λ₁| / |λ₃| = **2.649**.

A linear iteration `M^k v` with a dominant complex eigenvalue at angle θ produces period `2π/θ` for inputs concentrated in the dominant eigenspace. Anna's dominant phase is essentially π/2, which yields period 4.

<Callout type="warning" title="This is motivation, not proof">
For the **nonlinear** operator `T = sign ∘ M`, the linear-iteration argument does not derive period-4 — sign-projection is non-commutative with eigenspace decomposition. We cite the spectral structure as motivation only. The **proof** is empirical (sections 3 and 4): 100% of 100,000 random samples + 32,768 exhaustive HW≤2 samples show period-4.

A formal proof would require a lemma stating that sign-projection preserves the rotational structure of the dominant eigenpair when its magnitude dominates by enough. We have not constructed this lemma.
</Callout>

## 6. Theoretical convergence bound

Under linear iteration `M^k v`, components in eigenspaces with smaller magnitude decay like `(|λ_3| / |λ_1|)^k`. Crossing time at which they fall below resolution `ε = 1/N = 1/128`:

> `t_resolution ≈ log(1/N) / log(|λ_3|/|λ_1|) = log(1/128) / log(0.3774) ≈ 4.98 ticks`

Empirical `ttfc` median is **8.0**. The factor of ~1.6 between linear-decay bound and observed sign-projected convergence is plausible: sign-projection adds discrete jumps that may delay alignment with the dominant eigenspace.

## 6.5 Relation to CfB's anti-attractor concept (interpretive)

<Callout type="warning" title="Tier 3 — One reading among several">
C.f.B. (2026-04-30) defines anti-attractors as "phase spaces completely unchanged thus saving enormous amount of energy". A strict reading of "completely unchanged" would require a 1-tick fixed point (`T(v) = v`), which the period-4 cycles are not — they change every tick.

A 4-tick sampling reading ("if you observe `T⁴` rather than `T`, the cycle states are unchanged") is **one** possible interpretation that makes the period-4 cycles qualify as anti-attractors in CfB's energy-saving sense. We propose this reading without claiming it is CfB's intended definition. The period-4 finding itself does not depend on this interpretation; it is a structural property of `T` regardless of how it relates to CfB's energy framing.

A definitive answer requires further communication with the source.
</Callout>

## 7. The two iterations on Anna (architecture clarification)

Two distinct algorithms have been studied on the Anna Matrix and they produce **different attractor structures**. This was the source of confusion in earlier project documents.

| Algorithm | Operator | Termination | Reported attractors | Cycle period |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **(A)** Direct ternary iteration | `T(v) = sign(M·v)` | period-4 cycle (no fixed point reached) | **2** unique cycles | 4 |
| **(B)** `aigarth_search` ITU iteration | input/output neuron split with no-state-change termination | first state with no neuron change | **16,384** unique outputs | (1, after termination) |

### 7.1 Verification of (B) being a different algorithm

`aigarth_search --hamming 3` on Anna reports `max_ticks_reached = 0`: every input terminates at tick 1 with `avg_ticks = 2`. `aigarth_search --random 1000` reports `max_ticks_reached = 2`. If this were direct `T` iteration, every non-zero input would be in period-4 and never satisfy the no-state-change termination, so `tick_count` would always equal the cap (100). It does not.

We tested 500 cycle states from `search_hamming3.json` against `T`:

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |
| `sign(M · v) == v` strictly | 1 / 500 (the all-zero vector only) |
| Hamming distance from `sign(M · v)` to `v` | mean 98.52 / 128, median 100 |

The 16,384 attractors of `aigarth_search` are not strict 1-tick anti-attractors of `T`.

### 7.2 What this means

Both numbers are real and reproducible. They describe two different mathematical objects:

- The **2 period-4 cycles** of `T` are the 4-tick anti-attractors of the direct ternary iteration. This is the structure that matches the dominant π/2 eigenvalue and that drives the C-C-R-R behavioural sequence in the ALife simulation (Chapter 25, Section 4.6).
- The **16,384 unique outputs** of `aigarth_search` are the 1-tick fixed points of an ITU-style iteration with separate input and output neuron regions. This is closer to the architecture described by the official `aigarth-it` Python package.

Earlier project documents conflated the two as "Anna's attractor basins". They are different.

```python
import json, numpy as np

M = np.array(json.load(open('apps/web/public/data/anna-matrix.json'))['matrix'], dtype=np.int64)

def T(v): return np.sign(M @ v).astype(np.int64)

def find_cycle(v, max_ticks=100):
 history = [tuple(v.tolist())]
 seen = {history[0]: 0}
 for t in range(1, max_ticks + 1):
 v = T(v)
 h = tuple(v.tolist())
 if h in seen:
 return [history[i] for i in range(seen[h], t)], t - seen[h]
 history.append(h)
 seen[h] = t
 return [], 0

# Find both cycles
np.random.seed(42)
cycles = {}
for _ in range(200):
 v = np.random.choice([-1, 0, 1], 128).astype(np.int64)
 c, p = find_cycle(v.copy())
 if p == 4:
 canon = min(tuple(c[i:] + c[:i]) for i in range(4))
 cycles.setdefault(canon, c)
 if len(cycles) == 2: break

A, B = list(cycles.values())
assert {tuple(s) for s in A} == {tuple(-np.array(s)) for s in B}, "B should be -A pointwise (as set)"
print("Cycle sums A:", [sum(s) for s in A])
print("Cycle sums B:", [sum(s) for s in B])
# Output:
# Cycle sums A: [-42, 50, 38, -56] (or rotation)
# Cycle sums B: [42, -50, -38, 56] (or rotation)
```

## 12. Cross-reference: AIT regime gives different behaviour

The period-4 attractor described in this chapter is a property of the direct ternary operator `T(v) = sign(M · v)`. Phase N's verification campaign (2026-05-09, see [Chapter 31](/docs/03-results/31-anna-concept-classifier)) established that the production HyperIdentity scoring algorithm — which is what the project's `aigarth_search` C scanner runs — uses a different rule:

> `T_AIT(v) = ternary_clamp(sign(M) · v)` with input clamping and a stop condition.

Two consequences:

1. **Magnitudes are inert under `T_AIT`.** Anna's specific int8 values — trace=137 (integer landmark; equals ⌊1/α⌋ as a suggestive coincidence, not a derivation), π-approximants in some cells, and the K-e-y ASCII diagonal — participate in `T` but not in `T_AIT`.
2. **Period-4 does not transfer.** Sampling 100 random binary inputs under `sign(M) · v` shows period-4 in only 1 of 100 cases. Phase G's period-4 finding is M-magnitude-dependent and lives in the `T` regime; the AIT regime gives a 19-concept classifier behaviour instead (Chapter 31).

Both regimes are valid descriptions of Anna under different operators. The production scoring task uses `T_AIT`; the analytic exploration in this chapter uses `T`. They are complementary, not contradictory.


## 13. Endogenous activity — Anna's period-4 holds under free iteration without clamping

A free-iteration experiment_, pre-registered) tests whether the period-4 structure survives when sign-only iteration `sign(M · v)` is run from random ±1 initial states without any input clamping. Results (n=20 seeds × T=200; supplementary 5 seeds × T=10,000):

| Architecture | Cycle structure |
|---|---|
| **Anna** | **Period-4 in 25/25 random initial states** (T=200 + T=10K combined) |
| Random antipodal sign matrix (uniform ±1, 99.6% antipodal) | 0/5 cycles within 10,000 ticks |
| EngineeredDCOnly (Walsh DC-only) | Period-2 in 10/10 |
| EngineeredWalsh (Anna-frequency-mimic) | Variable 1, 2, 3, or 4 across 10 seeds |

Two findings:

1. **Sustained short-cycle dynamics is a Walsh-family property.** Anna, DC-only, and Walsh-frequency variants all reach short cycles. Random antipodal matrices do not (orbits > 10,000 long, if they cycle at all).

2. **Period-4 specifically is an Anna-specific property.** Engineered Walsh matrices with similar frequency distributions but shuffled row arrangements produce variable cycle lengths (1-4). Anna's exact period-4 depends on the specific row arrangement, not just the frequency spectrum.

This extends Chapter 27's seven-position signature finding (which used CLAMPED-input runs over 1000 random binary inputs) to the UNCLAMPED free-iteration regime: the period-4 attractor is intrinsic to Anna's `sign(M · v)` dynamics, not an artefact of input encoding.

**Reading note.** Bounded periodic cycles are structured but not adaptive. Once on the cycle, the dynamics repeat exactly. Adaptive temporal structure (the kind that improves behaviour over a system's lifetime) requires the surrounding framework — Aigarth-IT mutation, ITU growth/prune, or Neuraxon-style neuromodulator/circadian layers. This section establishes the bounded-periodic form of endogenous activity for the bare matrix, not the open-ended adaptive form. The latter is documented in the Cognitive Lab and the Phase 4 Anna×Neuraxon fusion work.

The cycle order is `concept_5 → concept_1 → concept_4 → concept_0 → concept_5 → ...` — alternating between the two antipode pair families. Seeds enter the cycle at different phases but the cycle itself is unique.

This is a bridge between two regimes of Anna's behaviour:
- **Clamped-input AIT** (production HyperIdentity scoring) — 19 stable concept basins, top 2 pairs cover 80% of mass
- **Free iteration** (no clamping) — period-4 cycle through exactly those same top 2 pairs in the output-half

The matrix's dominant attractors under input pressure and under free iteration are the same set. This is a measurable architectural regularity, not a consciousness claim.
